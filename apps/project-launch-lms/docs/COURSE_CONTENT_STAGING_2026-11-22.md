## 1. Course-level learner content

### Course description (home page)

**Project Launch Lab: From Curiosity to Action**

You don't need a perfect idea to begin something meaningful. In this short online course, you will explore interests and questions, compare possible projects, choose a direction that matters to you, and plan a small first move. You will practice making decisions with limited time and resources, consider feedback, and use evidence to decide what to try next.

The work stays yours. You do not need to spend money, contact strangers, share private information, make public posts, or use artificial intelligence. A safe, honest account of an obstacle can be more useful than pretending an experiment succeeded.

**Module 1 — Discover & Define:** Compare project directions using personal relevance, feasibility, and scope. Create a Project Direction Brief.

**Module 2 — Plan & Launch:** Identify planning components, study a worked example, practice a safe first step, and document evidence or a real barrier in a Launch Portfolio.

Each module includes instructional material, guided practice, a learning activity that produces something you can discuss with your facilitator, and a graded assignment with clear criteria. You can review the instruction again whenever you need it.

## 2. Module 1 — Discover & Define

**Student introduction / module landing:**

You may have several interests and questions worth exploring. This module will help you narrow those possibilities without losing the reasons they matter to you. You will learn three lenses—relevance, feasibility and scope—then observe an example, try guided decisions, and compare two possibilities for your own project. After feedback, you'll submit a Project Direction Brief. The goal is a reasoned choice, not a perfect answer.

**Module 1 learner-facing outcome summary:** By the end, you will recognize possible project starting points, explain how relevance, feasibility and scope affect choices, and justify a manageable project direction. The scored task is the Direction Brief, with the original 30-point rubric in the current LMS. The specific revised MLO wording and its graded-evidence mapping remain under faculty review.

### Assigned original companion reading — Three Lenses, One Decision

An interest is a starting point, not yet a project plan. “I like art,” for example, can lead to many directions: learning a drawing technique, making illustrations to explain a topic, or designing a small display for a school club. Your task is not to prove that one of these is objectively the best idea. It is to make a choice you can explain.

**Relevance:** Why does this direction matter to you or to a community you care about? Personal relevance can come from curiosity, usefulness, creativity, or a question you'd like to answer. A meaningful reason doesn't need to be dramatic.

**Feasibility:** What would it take to begin? Consider time, supplies, permissions, information and support. A project can matter a great deal and still be difficult to start right now. That doesn't make the idea bad; it may suggest a smaller first step.

**Scope:** How big is the idea? “Improve school lunches” is broad. “Ask five willing students which lunch menu information is hardest to find, without collecting sensitive personal details” is narrower and creates a specific question. Focus lets you gather evidence without claiming to solve the whole problem at once.

A **tradeoff** is something you accept when choosing one option over another. Maybe an idea is personally exciting but requires materials you don't have. Another idea may be less ambitious but easier to test. A strong decision recognizes both strengths and limitations.

**Try this:** Write two possible directions. For each, name one reason it matters, one constraint, and one way to narrow it. Which direction would you investigate first, and what could make you reconsider?

This text is an **original written instructional companion**. It does not replace the ten-screen worked lesson, independent multimedia requirement, Gauntlet, feedback activity or graded Brief.

### Assigned original companion reading — An Informative First Move

A first action does not need to complete a project. Its job is to help you learn something useful while staying safe and realistic.

Begin with your **purpose**: what are you trying to improve, create or understand? Next, identify **milestones**—at least three ordered stages that take you from preparation toward a possible outcome. A milestone tells you what progress could look like, not what must happen no matter what.

Then list **resources** and **constraints**. Resources might include time, information, a workspace or permission from a responsible adult. Constraints are things that may limit your action: a busy schedule, unavailable materials, privacy concerns or school rules. A thoughtful plan includes what you would do if a constraint matters.

Choose one **bounded action**. It should be small enough to do safely and informative enough to help you decide what comes next. For a school garden, the bounded action may be to ask a staff sponsor what permission is needed and sketch a possible single bed, not to start digging without permission.

Finally, distinguish **prediction** from **evidence**. “I think students will like my idea” is a prediction. “Three people I asked could explain the draft clearly” is an observation, though the observation still cannot prove that everyone will like it. Be specific about what you actually learned and what remains uncertain.

When feedback arrives, you do not have to agree automatically. Consider the comment, ask whether evidence supports it, and either revise your decision or explain why you are keeping it.

**Try this:** For a project you care about, write one safe first action, one thing that could stop it, one possible observation and one thing that observation would **not** prove.

**Module 2 conclusion — learner-facing copy:**

A useful first action is not necessarily one that succeeds. You may have learned from a small attempt, or you may have learned that a barrier needs to be addressed first.

Look at your original First Move Card, the feedback you received and your Launch Portfolio. Which part of your plan is now clearer? What is still uncertain? Choose one next step that makes sense based on the evidence available, not what you wish had happened.

The same planning questions can help with a different project: Why does it matter? What is manageable now? What could I observe? What would the observation not prove? Who could provide appropriate support?

You are leaving this course with a process you can reuse, not a requirement to finish a giant project in one sitting.
